## Problem

My WhatsApp groups (family, college friends, old teams) run on birthdays and festivals. Someone posts "Happy birthday!", ten people follow within the hour, and if I'm in a meeting or asleep I either miss it or turn up a day late. I wanted something that joins in for me, the way I would: once, warmly, addressed to the right person, and only when the group is actually celebrating.

That sounds like one LLM call per message, but that design fails in three ways:

- **Cost.** A busy group sends hundreds of messages a day, and almost none of them are wishes. Sending every message to a model costs money for nothing.
- **Wrong person.** A model reading "Happy birthday Lizzy!" will happily wish "Lizzy", even when I've never called her that, or wish someone who wasn't the birthday person at all.
- **Spam.** If the bot replies to every wish in a thread, wishes for me, or wishes the same person again an hour later, it's worse than not having a bot.

So the real problem was a pipeline: decide cheaply whether there's anything worth spending money on, and refuse to send anything it isn't sure about.

## Architecture

<Architecture />

Every message reaches the bot as a push over WhatsApp's multi-device WebSocket, through the open-source Baileys library. There's no polling and no headless browser. Each message then goes through a series of checks, cheapest first, and most of them stop it right there.

1. **Whitelisted group.** Only groups listed in `ALLOWED_GROUPS`, by name or group ID, count. Any other chat is ignored, with one exception: if I send `!stats` in my own chat, the bot replies there with its live metrics.
2. **Sent by someone else.** If the message is mine and it's a wish, I've already wished that person myself. The bot cancels any reply it had queued for that group, puts the person on cooldown, and sends nothing.
3. **Local pre-filter (01).** A list of regular expressions and emoji rules (`happy birthday`, `hbd`, `many happy returns`, `Saal Mubarak`, `🎂` bursts and so on) decides whether the text looks like a wish at all. It's free and runs in-process, so every message that isn't a wish stops here.
4. **45-minute sliding window.** Wishes are buffered per group. Nothing happens until at least two *different* people have wished within 45 minutes, so one person's message can't set the bot off.
5. **Gemini check (02).** This is the only step that costs money. Gemini reads the buffered wishes and decides what's being celebrated (a birthday, an anniversary or a festival), whether it's genuine, and whether the group is wishing *me*. If it says no, the bot doesn't wish, but it DMs me what it saw and why it held back. Calls are capped at 20 a day; past the cap, or if the API fails, a template-based rule engine takes over.
6. **Resolve who to tag.** A birthday needs exactly one person to address and an anniversary needs both partners, found from the messages' @mentions or my synced contacts. If the bot can't find them, it doesn't guess. It DMs me the original wishes so I can reply myself.
7. **Cooldown (03).** A person can be wished at most once per group every 48 hours. If they're on cooldown, the message is ignored.
8. **Human-like delay.** The reply waits a random 45 to 180 seconds and shows "typing…" for 3 seconds before it's sent.
9. **Send.** The bot checks the cooldown again, records it under every name and number the person goes by, sends the wish, and DMs me a one-line summary of what it said and where.

## Approach

### Push, not polling

Baileys speaks WhatsApp's linked-device protocol directly, so the bot is a small Node.js process rather than a browser automating WhatsApp Web. It runs in a Docker container on Railway with an always-restart policy. The session, contacts, cooldowns and metrics live on a mounted volume, so a redeploy doesn't log the bot out or forget who it wished. Linking a headless server to a phone is awkward, so the bot supports a QR code, an 8-character pairing code, or a session seeded from my laptop through an environment variable.

### Cheap checks first

Every stage in the pipeline is cheaper than the one after it. The regex pre-filter and the window are plain in-memory work, and they throw away nearly everything before the model sees it. In a busy group, the bill stays flat until people actually start wishing someone. The daily cap means a burst of festival wishes across many groups still has a ceiling. The model name defaults to `auto`: the bot lists the available Gemini models and picks the newest stable flash-lite one, so a model retirement doesn't take it down.

### Never tag the wrong person

The model helps decide *whether* to wish, but plain code decides *who*. After Gemini answers, the bot checks its targets against the @mentions in the messages and my own contact list:

```js
// Birthdays need one resolved person; anniversaries need both.
const requiredTargets = eventType === "anniversary" ? 2 : eventType === "birthday" ? 1 : 0;
if (requiredTargets > 0 && targetJids.length < requiredTargets) {
  return {
    shouldWish: false,
    unresolvedTarget: true, // the bot DMs me the original wishes instead
    reasoning: "Could not figure out who to @ …",
  };
}
```

The reply addresses each person by the name saved in my contacts and falls back to an @mention when there isn't one. Nicknames other people use in the chat never make it into the message, so the bot can't call someone "Lizzy" because a friend did.

### Cooldowns under every alias

The same person shows up as a WhatsApp ID, a bare phone number, my saved name for them and whatever the group calls them. A cooldown stored under only one of those wouldn't catch the others, so the bot records it under all of them. Cooldowns are scoped to a group and a person, which means two birthdays in the same group on the same day don't block each other. When I wish someone by hand, the bot records it the same way, so it never wishes after me.

## Results

The bot runs around the clock on Railway and joins in on birthday, anniversary and festival threads in my groups. Before a reply is sent, a thread has to get through every step above, and anything the bot isn't sure about comes to me as a DM instead of going to the group. Spending is bounded by design: at most 20 Gemini calls a day, with a template fallback after that.

The repo has a simulator, `npm run simulate`, that pushes made-up messages through the real pipeline without connecting to WhatsApp. The clip at the top of the page is that run. ## What I learned

- **Order the pipeline by cost.** The biggest saving came from the order of the checks, not from a smarter model. Once the free checks run first, the expensive one hardly ever runs.
- **Don't let the model decide who.** The model is good at "is this a genuine birthday thread?" and unreliable at "whose phone number is this?". Checking its answer against data I already hold (mentions and my contacts) removed a whole class of embarrassing mistakes.
- **A human fallback beats a guess.** When the bot isn't sure, it sends me a DM instead of posting in the group. A missed auto-wish costs nothing; a wish to the wrong person in a family group costs a lot.
- **Headless bots need observability.** A WebSocket that silently stops reconnecting looks exactly like a quiet day. A watchdog that flags a connection stuck down for over a minute, structured logs, and a `!stats` command I can send from my phone turned "is it even running?" into something I can check in a few seconds.
